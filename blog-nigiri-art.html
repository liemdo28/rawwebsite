The Art of Nigiri: A Chef's Perspective

Published February 10, 2025 | By Hoang, Founder | 10 min read

To the untrained eye, nigiri sushi appears deceptively simple: a slice of fish atop a small mound of rice. But this elegant simplicity belies a profound complexity that takes decades to master. In my twenty years as a sushi chef, I'm still discovering nuances in this ancient art form.

Today, I want to share what makes nigiri truly exceptional—the countless decisions, techniques, and traditions that transform two humble ingredients into something transcendent.

The Foundation: Shari (Sushi Rice)

In sushi, everything begins with rice. We call it shari, and it's the soul of every piece of nigiri. Many believe the fish is the star, but master sushi chefs know differently—the rice is everything.

The Rice Ritual

Every morning at Raw Sushi Bistro, our rice preparation begins at 5 AM. This isn't a task we delegate lightly; our most experienced chefs handle it because it's that important.

The Grain

We use premium short-grain Japanese rice with high starch content. The variety, growing region, and harvest season all affect the final product.

The Wash

Rice is washed 5-7 times until the water runs clear, removing excess starch while preserving the grain's integrity.

The Rest

After washing, rice rests for 30 minutes, allowing grains to absorb moisture evenly before cooking.

The Seasoning

While hot, rice is seasoned with our house-made blend of rice vinegar, sugar, and salt—a recipe perfected over twenty years.

The seasoning process, called shari-kiri, is almost meditative. We fold—never stir—the vinegar mixture into the rice using a cutting motion with a wooden paddle. Simultaneously, we fan the rice to cool it quickly, creating that characteristic subtle shine.

"Perfect sushi rice should be warm—about body temperature—with grains that hold together but separate easily on the tongue. Each grain should be distinct, yet the whole should have cohesion."

The Art of Neta: Selecting and Preparing Fish

While rice is the foundation, the fish—or neta—is where a chef's expertise truly shines. Fish selection requires knowledge accumulated over years, understanding not just species but seasons, regions, and individual specimens.

Reading the Fish

At the fish market, I assess dozens of factors in seconds:

  • Eyes: Should be clear and bright, never cloudy
  • Gills: Bright red indicates freshness
  • Flesh: Should spring back when pressed, never leaving an indentation
  • Smell: Fresh fish smells like clean ocean water, not "fishy"
  • Fat Content: Visible marbling indicates quality and season

The Cut: Mastering the Knife

How you cut fish for nigiri affects texture, flavor, and presentation. The angle, pressure, and motion of the knife all matter.

1 The Angle: Most fish is cut at a slight angle to create an elegant shape and maximize surface area. Tuna might be cut at 45 degrees, while delicate white fish requires a gentler angle.

2 The Motion: We use a pulling motion—one fluid movement from heel to tip of the knife. No sawing. A single, confident stroke.

3 The Thickness: Generally 5-8mm, but this varies by fish. Fatty tuna can be thicker; lean fish like sea bream is cut thinner to enhance its delicate texture.

4 Against the Grain: We always cut perpendicular to the muscle fibers, ensuring the fish has the right texture on the palate.

The Assembly: Where Art Meets Science

Now comes the moment where everything converges: forming the nigiri. This is where years of training become evident.

The Five Movements (Te-gaeshi)

Traditional nigiri is formed with five precise hand movements. It looks effortless, but achieving this fluidity takes years:

Movement 1: Take a small amount of rice—about 18 grams—and form a rough oblong shape

Movement 2: Apply wasabi to the fish with your right index finger

Movement 3: Place rice on fish, gently pressing with your right index and middle fingers

Movement 4: Flip with your left hand while your right shapes the sides

Movement 5: Final press from the top, creating the characteristic nigiri shape

The entire process takes 3-4 seconds. The rice should be just compact enough to hold shape but loose enough to fall apart gently in your mouth.

The Pressure Point

How firmly you press the rice is crucial. Too loose, and it falls apart before reaching your mouth. Too tight, and it becomes a dense, unpleasant mouthful that doesn't release its flavors properly.

We call the perfect pressure fuwa-fuwa—fluffy yet cohesive. It should yield at the slightest bite, allowing fish and rice to blend harmoniously.

Temperature: The Hidden Variable

One of the most overlooked aspects of great nigiri is temperature. At Raw, we're meticulous about this:

Rice Temperature

Always served at body temperature (98.6°F / 37°C), which brings out the rice's subtle sweetness and allows it to meld with the fish.

Fish Temperature

Slightly cooler than rice but not cold. Fish that's too cold loses flavor and has an unpleasant texture.

The Contrast

The slight temperature difference between rice and fish creates a pleasant sensory experience that enhances flavor perception.

Beyond Technique: The Intangibles

Technical mastery is essential, but truly exceptional nigiri requires something more—qualities that can't be taught, only cultivated:

Intuition

After twenty years, my hands know what my mind is still processing. The moment I touch a fish, I know how to cut it. When I pick up rice, my hands automatically form the right amount. This intuition comes only from repetition—tens of thousands of pieces of nigiri.

Respect

Every fish gave its life for our craft. This isn't metaphorical—it's deeply felt. When I prepare nigiri, I'm honoring that sacrifice. This respect translates into care, attention, and a determination to waste nothing and showcase everything.

Connection

The best nigiri is made for someone, not just served to them. When you sit at our sushi bar, I'm not just forming rice and fish—I'm reading you, understanding what you'll appreciate, adjusting thickness, pressure, and presentation based on the conversation we're having.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

For those aspiring to appreciate or create great nigiri, here are pitfalls to avoid:

  • Over-dipping in soy sauce: A light touch is enough—you want to taste the fish, not drown it
  • Eating it cold: Nigiri should never be refrigerated. It should be eaten within moments of preparation
  • Adding too much wasabi: The chef has already added the perfect amount between fish and rice
  • Separating fish from rice: They're meant to be eaten together, in one bite if possible
  • Overthinking it: Yes, there's artistry and technique, but ultimately, trust your palate and enjoy

The Journey Never Ends

I've been making nigiri for over twenty years, yet I'm still learning. A new technique observed during a trip to Tokyo. A subtle adjustment to our rice recipe based on the season. A different way of cutting sea bream that better showcases its texture.

This is what I love about sushi—it's an art form that rewards lifelong dedication. There's always another level of refinement, another subtle improvement to discover.

"The day you think you've mastered nigiri is the day you stop being a sushi chef. True mastery is understanding that you'll never fully master it—and finding joy in that eternal pursuit."
